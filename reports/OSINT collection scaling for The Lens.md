# Unlimited collection is a myth — build around it

"Unlimited" refresh rate and volume across X, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook and Reddit does not exist as a legitimate option for a solo consultancy in 2026, at any price you can actually afford, and the research is unambiguous about why: every one of those platforms either gates its real-time firehose behind a "contact sales" enterprise contract with no published floor, or closes its highest-volume API entirely to commercial users and reserves it for academic researchers. That is the honest headline. But it is not the useful part of the answer, because The Lens does not need Twitter-firehose-grade volume to be a serious African security-monitoring platform — it needs dramatically more throughput than it currently gets from the sources it already has rights to, and that is almost entirely a software problem, not a procurement problem. Cloudflare's 2026 platform limits (10,000 subrequests per invocation on paid plans, Queues fan-out to 250 concurrent consumers) mean the Lens's existing architecture is running at a small fraction of its own ceiling; GDELT alone ships three parallel 15-minute-refresh bulk products and Simon is using one; Telegram's actual constraint on broad channel monitoring is a missing MTProto login, not a platform limit; and the free structured-data layer (UCDP, FEWS NET, IPC, Copernicus, FIRMS) is both under-ingested and, critically, legally cleaner than ACLED, whose commercial EULA is the single biggest licensing trap in this whole space. The prioritized path below is: fix the architecture this month at near-zero cost, add the free-but-licensed structured sources this quarter with ACLED flagged for direct legal follow-up before touching it commercially, and treat paid social APIs and OSINT vendors as a later-stage decision gated by revenue, not a now-move.

## Why "unlimited" social media access does not exist at any price you can reach

Each closed platform researched has converged on the same structure — a token self-serve tier with a disclosed price, and a true high-volume tier that is unpriced and sales-gated — but the specifics differ enough to matter for planning. **X/Twitter** runs a legacy ladder (Basic ~$200/month, Pro reported at roughly $5,000/month or $54,000/year in 2024-era figures) plus, as of an October 2025 beta, a new pay-per-use/credit model; above that sits a fully custom Enterprise line — Volume streams (the firehose), Powerstream (filtered streaming), and Decahose — priced only via "contact sales for high-volume pricing," with no public 2026 figure found in this research; historical Gnip-era firehose deals ran tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands per month, but that is an unconfirmed, dated estimate, not a current quote ([X Enterprise API Pricing docs](https://docs.x.com/enterprise-api/getting-started/pricing); [TechCrunch, Oct 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/21/x-is-testing-a-pay-per-use-pricing-model-for-its-api/)). **Reddit** has no published commercial price at all — its Developer Platform support page states plainly that "select developers who require broader access... may be charged fees," and commercial use requires "obtain[ing] permission and sign[ing] a contract" through an enterprise request form ([Reddit Developer Platform](https://support.reddithelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/14945211791892-Developer-Platform-Accessing-Reddit-Data)); the often-repeated "~$12,000/month" figure circulating in developer forums could **not** be confirmed from any primary source and should be treated as unverified. Reddit's 2024 Public Content Policy also contractually bars licensees from using the data for "surveillance" — a term a conflict-monitoring consultancy cannot assume excludes its own use case without asking Reddit directly ([TechCrunch, May 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/09/reddit-locks-down-its-public-data-in-new-content-policy-says-use-now-requires-a-contract)). **Meta** and **TikTok** are not expensive — they are categorically closed. Meta's Content Library and API (the CrowdTangle successor) is restricted to "qualified institutions pursuing scientific or public interest research," routed through academic partners like ICPSR, with no commercial pathway mentioned anywhere in the sourced material ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/meta-provides-expanded-access-content-library-api/700505/)). TikTok's Research API requires applicants to be "independent of commercial interests" and conduct "not-for-profit or non-commercial" research — Afrilens is explicitly excluded by TikTok's own eligibility language, and no commercial-equivalent high-volume monitoring product was confirmed to exist ([TikTok Research Tools](https://developers.tiktok.com/products/research-api/)). Instagram and Facebook's organic-content coverage sits behind that same closed Content Library door; only the narrower Ad Library API (paid ads, not organic posts) remains open without gatekeeping.

What this means concretely: there is no checkbox Simon can tick, no API key he can request this month, that gives The Lens comprehensive, high-refresh, legitimate coverage of X, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook or Reddit. The realistic legitimate paths into those platforms are (a) X's disclosed self-serve tiers for narrow, filtered, moderate-volume monitoring, (b) an eventual commercial social-listening vendor that has already negotiated its own platform data-licensing deals (see the paid-options section), or (c) in Reddit's case, a direct licensing conversation with no public price anchor. None of these constitute "unlimited," and all of the vendor-mediated options are priced for organizations far larger than a solo consultancy.

## Cloudflare's own ceiling is far above where The Lens is running today

The single highest-leverage, lowest-cost move available is architectural, not financial. Cloudflare removed its historical 1,000-subrequest-per-invocation cap; **paid-plan Workers now get 10,000 subrequests per invocation by default, configurable up to 10 million** ([Cloudflare Changelog, Feb 2026](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-02-11-subrequests-limit/)). That single change means the platform-level justification for throttling a tick to a handful of sources no longer exists — the real bottleneck is the "one Durable Object processes the whole batch serially" pattern the pipeline currently uses, not Cloudflare's request ceiling. Durable Objects carry a soft limit of roughly 1,000 requests/second each but impose no cap on how many DO instances can exist, while **Cloudflare Queues supports up to 250 concurrent push-based consumer invocations per queue**, 5,000 messages/second throughput, and batching up to 100 messages ([Cloudflare Queues limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/platform/limits/)). The concrete redesign: instead of a Cron trigger invoking one Durable Object that marches through ~15 of roughly 260 RSS sources per tick, the Cron handler should enqueue all ~260 source URLs as individual Queue messages and let up to 250 parallel consumer Workers each fetch one feed and write to D1. That turns a full-corpus refresh cycle from "many hours" into "one batch window of seconds to low minutes" — a genuine order-of-magnitude throughput gain using infrastructure Simon already pays for, with **medium engineering effort** (a queue producer, a consumer handler, and migrating existing fetch/parse logic into it — likely days, not weeks). The same logic applies to the GDELT live-query connector: the self-imposed 20-requests-per-5-minutes budget was calibrated against the old Cloudflare subrequest ceiling, not against any documented GDELT server-side limit, so it should be titrated upward experimentally (watching for 429/503 responses) rather than assumed fixed — **low effort**, essentially a config change plus monitoring.

Two sources of volume are sitting unused inside products Simon already ingests. **GDELT 2.0 ships three parallel bulk-file products on the same 15-minute refresh cycle** — Event Export (already in use), the Global Knowledge Graph (GKG, which adds thematic, tone, and casualty/displacement "counts" data), and the Mentions table (which tracks repeat media coverage of an event, a proxy for amplification/resonance) — all as the same unthrottled static-file downloads already in the pipeline, with **zero additional rate-limit exposure** ([GDELT Project data page](https://gdeltproject.org/data.html); [GDELT 2.0 announcement](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/)). Adding GKG and Mentions ingestion is **low-to-medium effort** — same fetch/schedule pattern, new schema to parse — and multiplies both volume and analytical depth without touching a single rate limit. GDELT also already runs a 65-language live-translation layer (GDELT Translingual) feeding its Event/GKG streams, meaning francophone and arabophone African media are already captured without Simon building anything new — worth confirming in practice, but it is a capability already paid for by nobody. Separately, **Telegram's actual constraint on monitoring "hundreds" of public channels is architectural, not legal**: the Bot API cannot proactively read arbitrary public channels it hasn't been added to, while MTProto client libraries (Telethon, Pyrogram, GramJS) authenticate as a real logged-in user account and access exactly what the official Telegram app does — this is Telegram's own intended mechanism for reading public channels, and "using it is not itself a violation... what triggers restrictions is behaviour: high volume, identical messages to strangers, and recipient reports," none of which apply to read-only monitoring ([zupai.io: Telethon vs Bot API](https://zupai.io/blog/telethon-vs-telegram-bot-api-for-outreach)). Telegram publishes no numeric FLOOD_WAIT table, so scaling from today's unauthenticated web-preview approach to a real MTProto account means joining channels gradually and backing off on `FLOOD_WAIT_X` responses empirically rather than against a documented budget. The one real architecture wrinkle: an MTProto session is long-lived and stateful, which doesn't map cleanly onto Workers' stateless execution model — this piece likely needs a small always-on process outside Workers (a lightweight VM or container) that periodically pushes parsed channel data into D1 via the Workers API. Call this **medium-to-high effort**, the most substantial lift in this section, because it's new infrastructure outside Cloudflare, not a Workers-native change.

Finally, **Workers AI's `m2m100-1.2b` translation model runs inside Simon's existing Cloudflare billing at $0.342 per million input and output tokens**, with no new vendor relationship, API key, or egress integration ([Cloudflare Workers AI docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/models/m2m100-1.2b/)) — this is the natural default for translating African-language local press at volume, letting The Lens ingest Swahili, Hausa, Amharic and francophone sources it currently can't parse. External alternatives (Azure at $10/million characters, Google at $20/million, DeepL at roughly $25–27.50/million) are all cheaper-than-each-other but are all new paid vendors; Workers AI is the only option of the four that adds zero new vendor relationships, making it the right default to prototype, with the caveat that m2m100 is a 2020-era model and its quality on lower-resource African languages is unverified and should be spot-checked against real press text before relying on it for casualty figures or actor names. **This is low effort to prototype** (one Workers AI binding) and should be tested this month.

## Free structured data is underused — and ACLED's licensing trap needs resolving before anything else

Four free, high-quality structured sources have confirmed or strongly-implied no commercial-use restriction and real African depth: **UCDP** (free of charge, 5,000 requests/day API cap, Georeferenced Event Dataset with coordinates — [UCDP API docs](https://ucdp.uu.se/apidocs/)); **NASA FIRMS** (5,000 transactions per 10-minute window, confirmed African country-level queries for active-fire detection, useful as a proxy for scorched-earth tactics and displacement-camp fires — [FIRMS API guide](https://firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/content/academy/data_api/firms_api_use.html)); **Copernicus/Sentinel** satellite imagery, where the Legal Notice explicitly confirms the core Sentinel data is "free, full and open" for commercial use, distinct from the more restrictive terms covering only "other contents of the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem portal" ([Copernicus Terms and Conditions](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/terms-and-conditions)); and GDELT's full product suite described in the section above. IPC/Cadre Harmonisé (food-security classifications covering 30+ countries, 45+ for the West Africa/Sahel-focused CH component) and FEWS NET (USAID-run, IPC-compatible, with a third-party daily mirror suggesting it tolerates repeated automated polling) both look promising but their specific commercial-use terms were not confirmed in this research pass and need a direct terms-of-use read before high-volume ingestion ([IPC API](https://www.ipcinfo.org/ipc-country-analysis/api/); [FEWS NET API](https://help.fews.net/fdw/fews-net-api)).

**ACLED is the one source on this entire list that needs to be flagged in bold before anything else: its EULA explicitly states that "commercial entities may not access or use the Content and/or Platforms without first obtaining a corporate license,"** and separately prohibits using ACLED content "to develop, train, support, or assist in the development of any product or service that is competitive with Licensor's products or services" ([ACLED EULA](https://acleddata.com/eula); [ACLED Content Usage Terms](https://acleddata.com/contentusage)). A crisis-monitoring platform that ingests ACLED event data and repackages it for paying clients is exactly the use case that clause exists to control. If The Lens is currently pulling ACLED data under a free/registered account for a commercial product, that is very likely a license violation, not a gray area — ACLED does not publish commercial pricing, so this requires a direct email to ACLED's licensing contact before any further ingestion, not a workaround. HDX/HAPI, ACAPS, and ReliefWeb all host valuable datasets but their specific licensing text was not retrievable in this pass (one fetch was blocked with a 403) and should be confirmed per-dataset, since HDX aggregates content from many organizations under varying licenses rather than one blanket terms-of-use.

## Paid tiers above the free layer, honestly bucketed by confidence

Three distinct price bands exist above the free/structured-data layer, and the notes let us separate confirmed primary-source numbers from secondary estimates. The cheapest, most confidently-priced band is **developer-first news APIs**, all confirmed from vendor pricing pages: NewsCatcher Starter at **$50/month** (6,000 credits, 1-month search depth), Event Registry/NewsAPI.ai's 5K Plan at **$90/month** (covering roughly 500,000 articles across 150,000+ outlets in 50+ languages), and Perigon Basic at **$250/month** (10,000 requests/month, limited commercial license) scaling to Perigon Plus at $550/month or a custom Commercial tier starting at $24,000/year ([NewsCatcher Pricing](https://www.newscatcherapi.com/pricing); [NewsAPI.ai Plans](https://newsapi.ai/plans); [Perigon Pricing](https://perigon.io/products/pricing/apis)). None of these cover Telegram, dark web, or closed social platforms — they are news/web-text aggregators — but they are real, confirmed, non-trial prices a solo consultancy can budget against today.

The middle band is **enterprise OSINT/social-listening vendors**, where pricing is sparser and mostly secondary-sourced. The only two vendors with any confirmed low-entry price are **Talkwalker at $9,000/year** (its entry "Listening" plan, explicitly marketed to individuals and small teams — [SpotSaaS](https://www.spotsaas.com/product/talkwalker/pricing)) and **Meltwater**, whose nominal Starter is around $10,000/year but whose real tracked contracts cluster near $25,800/year with a typical range of $10,000–$130,000+ ([Octolens](https://octolens.com/blog/meltwater-pricing)). Everything else in this tier — Dataminr ($50,000–$200,000+/year, confirmed via a pricing-aggregator), Recorded Future ($40,000–$500,000+ depending on module count), ZeroFox (median buyer pays $45,604/year across 38 tracked purchases), Babel Street, Flashpoint, and ShadowDragon — clusters at $30,000 to $500,000+/year and is sales-led with no public self-serve signup ([ITQlick](https://www.itqlick.com/dataminr/pricing); [UnderDefense](https://underdefense.com/blog/recorded-future-pricing-guide/); [Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/zerofox)). Notably, **no vendor in this tier was found to market dedicated African-language or Africa-specific coverage** — Babel Street's "200+ languages / 95+ countries" is the closest general claim, but nothing confirms African-language depth specifically, which is a real market gap for Afrilens's positioning, not just a research miss.

The top band is **true platform-enterprise/firehose deals** — X's custom Enterprise Volume Streams/Powerstream/Decahose, Reddit's negotiated commercial contract, and the handful of OSINT vendors (Flashpoint, Babel Street) with genuine dark-web/Telegram-at-scale coverage. None of these publish numbers; all require "contact sales," and the only pricing anchors available are historical, unconfirmed, and from a different market era (pre-2023 Gnip firehose pricing in the tens-of-thousands-to-low-hundreds-of-thousands-per-month range). Treat this band as effectively out of reach for a solo operation right now — not because the research proves it's unaffordable, but because there is no number to budget against, and the sales-led process itself (calls, procurement cycles, annual contracts) is a cost a one-person consultancy has to absorb before even learning the price.

## A do-this-first list sized to one engineer and a Cloudflare account

This month, with zero new spend: rebuild the RSS ingestion path around Cloudflare Queues (Cron enqueues ~260 source URLs, up to 250 concurrent consumers fetch and write to D1) — this is the single highest-leverage change available and uses infrastructure already paid for; add GDELT GKG and Mentions ingestion alongside the existing Event Export pipeline, reusing the same fetch/schedule code against a new schema; and titrate the GDELT DOC 2.0 live-query budget upward past the old 20-requests-per-5-minutes ceiling, watching for 429/503 responses as the real signal rather than a guessed number. In parallel, send ACLED's licensing contact a direct email about commercial terms before any further ACLED ingestion continues — this is not optional homework, it's risk containment for a practice already likely in violation if ACLED data is being used commercially today.

This quarter: stand up a real MTProto-authenticated Telegram account (Telethon or GramJS) to replace the unauthenticated web-preview approach, running as a small always-on process outside the Workers runtime that feeds parsed channel data into D1 — join channels gradually and build exponential backoff against `FLOOD_WAIT` responses rather than assuming a safe request rate; prototype Workers AI's m2m100 translation on real Swahili/Hausa/Amharic/French press samples to validate quality before trusting it with casualty or actor-name details; and pull the actual terms-of-use text for HDX/HAPI, ACAPS, ReliefWeb, FEWS NET and IPC/Cadre Harmonisé — all five have dedicated terms pages referenced but not fully read in this research pass, and closing that gap is cheap (an afternoon of reading) relative to the ingestion value these sources unlock. If ACLED's commercial license turns out to be affordable, budget for it here; if not, UCDP becomes the primary structured-conflict-event backbone instead.

Later, only if the business grows enough to justify sales-cycle overhead and five-figure-plus annual spend: evaluate one of the developer-first news APIs (NewsCatcher at $50/month or Event Registry at $90/month are the cheapest real entry points) to supplement GDELT with structured, entity-tagged news text; and only once there's a client or revenue case that justifies it, have the "contact sales" conversation with Talkwalker ($9,000/year, the cheapest confirmed enterprise-tier entry point) or a similar vendor for broader multi-platform social coverage — go in explicitly asking whether they have any African-language source depth, since none of the twelve enterprise vendors researched confirmed this, and it may be a point of leverage in negotiation rather than something to assume exists. X's Enterprise firehose, Reddit's commercial contract, and any dark-web/Telegram-at-scale vendor (Flashpoint, Babel Street) belong in a "when a client is specifically paying for that coverage" bucket, not a general infrastructure roadmap — there's no public price to plan around, and the sales process itself costs more of a solo operator's time than the infrastructure work above.

## Legal guardrails, in practical terms

The case law gives a clear, narrow safe lane: **collection that is public, logged-out, and not verbatim-republished is the strongest position US courts currently recognize.** Both Meta v. Bright Data (January 2024) and X Corp v. Bright Data (2024) resulted in platforms losing breach-of-contract claims specifically because the scraper never logged in or used an account — the courts held that anti-scraping terms bind "users" exercising account privileges, not anonymous visitors reading public pages ([JD Supra on Meta v. Bright Data](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-1470808/); [Eric Goldman on X v. Bright Data](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/05/x-corp-v-bright-data-is-the-decision-weve-been-waiting-for-guest-blog-post.htm)). The moment an account, fake or real, gets involved, that protection disappears completely: hiQ v. LinkedIn "won" the CFAA question but ultimately settled in December 2022 with a permanent injunction, forced destruction of its scraped data and algorithms, and a $500,000 payment, after a court found it had breached LinkedIn's user agreement by using automated and crowdsourced fake accounts ([ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)); LinkedIn has continued winning decisively against scrapers using fake accounts as recently as a 2026 consent judgment against ProAPIs/Netswift. The practical rule for The Lens: never create or use fake accounts, never log in to automate collection past what a platform's own ToS permits for that login, and stay on public no-login surfaces (Telegram's own logged-in-user-reading-public-channels model is fine because it mirrors the official app's intended use, not an evasion of it; RSS and GDELT are inherently public and unauthenticated). None of this protects against a platform's own technical enforcement — rate-limiting, IP blocking, account suspension remain fully available to platforms regardless of litigation outcomes, and are a business-continuity risk independent of legal exposure.

The GDPR/Kenya angle is a separate, stricter layer that the US case law does not touch. EU regulatory guidance explicitly rejects "it's public, so it's fair game" as a defense — a Dutch DPA position states scraping personal data is "almost always unlawful under the GDPR" because the needed consent is rarely obtainable, and legitimate interest "cannot justify scraping done for the sole purpose of making money" ([Aphaia](https://aphaia.co.uk/web-scraping-is-almost-always-unlawful-under-the-gdpr/)) — directly relevant if The Lens ever stores or republishes identifiable personal data (names, faces, handles tied to individuals) from social posts to clients with EU exposure. Kenya's own Data Protection Act 2019 imposes an independent, GDPR-like obligation regardless of what any foreign platform's ToS says: every data controller or processor must register with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner before processing (non-registration is a criminal offense), identify a lawful basis under Section 30, and follow cross-border transfer rules under Section 48, with penalties up to KES 5 million or 1% of annual turnover ([Dimeri.ai summary](https://www.dimeri.ai/blog/guides/data-protection-act-kenya)). This is a compliance item for Afrilens as a Kenya-based controller independent of the scraping question entirely — the practical guardrail is to design The Lens around aggregated, structured event data and sourced facts rather than verbatim republication of individual posts or personally-identifiable content, and to confirm ODPC registration status as a baseline, separate from any platform-access decision above.

## Conclusion

The research converges on an uncomfortable but useful reframe: the limiting factor on The Lens's coverage right now is not platform access policy, it's a pipeline that processes 15 of 260 sources per tick on infrastructure rated for orders of magnitude more throughput. Fixing that — Queues-based fan-out, the two unused GDELT products, a real Telegram login, Workers AI translation — is available this month at the cost of engineering time Simon already has, and it almost certainly produces more usable signal than any single paid social API tier would, because those tiers (X Pro at ~$5,000/month, Talkwalker at $9,000/year) are narrow and filtered, not the comprehensive coverage "unlimited" implies. The one real compliance debt on the books is ACLED: if it's being ingested commercially today without a corporate license, that's not a future risk to plan around, it's a present one to resolve directly with ACLED this month, independent of everything else in this report.
