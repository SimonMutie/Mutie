# Scaling The Lens's Ingestion Pipeline on Cloudflare (Workers/DO/D1/Cron) Without New Paid Vendors

## Cloudflare platform limits relevant to throughput (Cron, Durable Objects, Queues, subrequests, CPU)

### Takeaway
Cloudflare's 2026 limits no longer cap subrequests at 1,000 — paid-plan Workers now get 10,000 subrequests per invocation by default (raisable to 10M), which alone removes the main technical reason to throttle GDELT or RSS fetches inside a single invocation. Durable Objects are the actual bottleneck for a "march through all sources on one tick" design (soft 1,000 req/s per object, serialized by nature), while Cloudflare Queues offers push-based fan-out to up to 250 concurrent consumer invocations per queue, which is the realistic path to real parallelism beyond one DO's serial loop.

### Cited Findings
- Workers subrequest limit: the historical hard cap of 1,000 subrequests per invocation has been removed; **paid plans now get 10,000 subrequests per invocation, configurable up to 10 million**; free plans remain at 50 external + 1,000 Cloudflare-service subrequests per invocation — [Cloudflare Changelog, 2026-02-11](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-02-11-subrequests-limit/), confirmed on [Workers Limits docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/)
- Cron Triggers: maximum of 5 triggers/account on Free, **250 triggers/account on Paid**; each invocation has a 15-minute wall-time ceiling; CPU time per cron invocation is 30 seconds (paid, for sub-1-hour interval triggers) or up to 15 minutes for triggers firing ≥1 hour apart — [Workers Limits docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/)
- The official limits page does not state an explicit minimum Cron interval number; third-party guides specifically discuss "Cloudflare Workers Cron Triggers: limits, minimum interval" as a known pain point, implying the platform's minimum cron granularity (commonly understood as 1 minute, standard 5-field cron syntax) is itself a hard floor that can't be pushed below — [crontap.com](https://crontap.com/blog/cloudflare-workers-cron-minute-limit) (third-party, not Cloudflare-primary; treat as corroborating, not authoritative)
- Workers CPU time: Free plan is capped at 10ms per request; Paid plan defaults to 30 seconds, adjustable up to 5 minutes for HTTP-triggered requests — [Workers Limits docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/)
- Durable Objects: each individual DO instance has a **soft limit of ~1,000 requests/second**; beyond that it returns overload errors; there is no cap on the *number* of DO instances you can create in a namespace; alarm handlers have the same 15-minute wall-time ceiling as other invocations; the docs do not publish a distinct "alarm concurrency" number — [Durable Objects limits docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/limits/) (fetched via proxy summary; could not find an explicit number of alarms-per-namespace-that-can-fire-concurrently, flagged as a gap below)
- Cloudflare Queues: **10,000 queues per account**; **5,000 messages/second throughput per queue**; max message size 128KB; batches capped at 100 messages or 256KB; **up to 250 concurrent push-based consumer invocations**; consumer wall-clock max 15 minutes, CPU time configurable up to 5 minutes; backlog capacity 25GB/queue; retention up to 14 days (24h on free); up to 100 retries/message — [Cloudflare Queues limits docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/platform/limits/)
- Durable Objects + Queues integration: Cloudflare's own engineering blog describes re-architecting Queues itself on top of Durable Objects to get a "10x speedup," explicitly framing DOs as fast when used correctly and Queues as the mechanism for scaling background/async job processing at the edge — [Cloudflare blog: "Durable Objects aren't just durable, they're fast"](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-we-built-cloudflare-queues/)

### Inferences
- The 5-minute Cron trigger is **not** the throughput ceiling; it's an orchestration cadence. The real serial bottleneck is the "one Durable Object does the whole batch in its own invocation" pattern — a single DO can be pushed harder (more subrequests per invocation now that 10,000/invocation is available on Paid, vs. being self-limited to 15 sources/tick), but it's still fundamentally one execution context processing sequentially (or with limited internal `Promise.all` fan-out) per tick.
- Swapping the current design for **Cron Trigger → enqueue all ~260 source URLs as individual Queue messages → many parallel Queue consumer Workers (up to 250 concurrent invocations) each fetching 1 feed and writing to D1** would let a full 260-source cycle complete in roughly one batch-processing window (seconds-to-low-minutes) instead of "many hours," because concurrency moves from 1 (one DO/tick) to up to 250 (concurrent consumers), each handling small batches (up to 100 messages but realistically 1-per-invocation-for-latency or small batches for efficiency).
- This is a genuine architecture change, not a bigger timer: Cron still fires every 5 minutes (or could stay the same), but instead of the Cron handler processing 15 feeds itself, it would just enqueue (or re-enqueue) jobs and let the Queue's consumer concurrency do the parallel work. Each consumer Worker invocation is cheap (fetch, parse, D1 write) and well within CPU budgets.
- For GDELT's live-query connector, the 20-requests/5-min self-imposed budget is a guess at an "undocumented" limit — given the Workers subrequest ceiling is no longer the constraint (10,000/invocation on Paid), the actual limiting factor is GDELT's server-side tolerance, not Cloudflare's platform. This argues for titrating the GDELT budget upward experimentally (watch for 429s/503s) rather than assuming Cloudflare forces a low number.

### Gaps
- Could not find an official Cloudflare-published explicit "alarm concurrency per Durable Object namespace" number — unclear whether many DO instances' alarms can all fire at effectively the same wall-clock time without platform-level queuing/delay.
- Could not find an authoritative Cloudflare statement of the literal minimum Cron Trigger interval (e.g., confirmation that 1-minute cron syntax is honored); only third-party blog evidence that minimum-interval behavior is a known friction point worth blog posts.
- Did not verify current D1 write-throughput limits (rows/sec, batch insert limits) which would also gate how fast a parallelized fan-out could land results — worth checking `developers.cloudflare.com/d1/platform/limits/` directly if D1 becomes the bottleneck after fan-out.

## Superfeedr and push-based RSS alternatives (avoiding polling loops entirely)

### Takeaway
Superfeedr is a hosted PubSubHubbub (WebSub) hub/aggregator: you give it feed URLs, it polls them on your behalf (at whatever cadence it determines, often sub-minute for active feeds) and pushes new entries to a webhook callback URL the moment they appear, eliminating Simon's own polling loop for any feed it manages; billing is usage-based with free coverage for small subscription counts, but exact current per-feed pricing beyond "small is free" could not be pinned down from available sources.

### Cited Findings
- Superfeedr "monitors feeds you subscribe to and pushes all new content to you immediately" via webhook callback rather than requiring you to poll — [Superfeedr Subscriber page](https://superfeedr.com/subscriber)
- Superfeedr normalizes incoming feed entries into Atom or JSON format regardless of source feed format, removing the need to handle heterogeneous RSS/Atom parsing per source — [Superfeedr Subscriber page](https://superfeedr.com/subscriber)
- Pricing is usage-based; Superfeedr states subscriptions under a threshold are free ("if you subscribed to less than 10 feeds in the month, your bill is on the house"), with cost scaling by max concurrent subscription count per 30-day billing period, and volume discounts for very high-volume accounts; **no fixed per-feed dollar figure was found in the fetched content** — [Superfeedr Subscriber/pricing page](https://superfeedr.com/subscriber)
- Superfeedr is built on and markets itself as "PubSubHubbub with benefits" — i.e., a WebSub-compliant hub that adds normalization, reliability and polling-on-your-behalf on top of the open W3C-descended WebSub spec — [Superfeedr blog](https://blog.superfeedr.com/pubsubhubbub.html)
- WebSub (formerly PubSubHubbub) is an open, standardized protocol: a publisher declares a hub in its feed, subscribers register a callback URL with that hub, and the hub pushes updates — meaning Simon isn't locked only to Superfeedr; any WebSub-compliant hub, or self-hosting a hub, is architecturally equivalent — [Wikipedia: WebSub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WebSub)

### Inferences
- Since 260 African news sources is well above Superfeedr's "under 10 feeds = free" line, this would move from "self-imposed throttling" to "a small new recurring bill" — which conflicts with the stated constraint of **no new paid data vendor**. Superfeedr is therefore **not a zero-cost fit** for the full 260-source corpus under the stated constraint, though it could be evaluated later as an optional paid upgrade, or applied selectively (e.g., free tier covering the ~9 highest-priority sources) without violating "no new paid vendor" if kept under the free threshold.
- A WebSub/PubSubHubbub architecture is appealing even if Simon doesn't use Superfeedr commercially: **most African news sites likely do not declare a `<link rel="hub">` in their RSS feeds** (WebSub requires publisher-side opt-in), so coverage would be partial regardless of hub choice — this needs per-source verification, not assumed.
- Given the "no new paid vendor" constraint, the Cloudflare Queues fan-out approach (above) is the more directly applicable lever for RSS specifically; Superfeedr-style push is a good idea to flag for Simon as a future/optional enhancement rather than a now-move.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve a concrete current Superfeedr price list (e.g., $/month at 50, 100, 500, 1000 feeds) — the fetched page described the free-tier framing but not a rate card; would need to check Superfeedr's actual pricing page content more directly (the fetch may have been summarized/truncated) or contact sales.
- Did not verify what fraction of the ~260 African RSS sources actually declare a WebSub hub (publisher-side requirement) — without this, neither Superfeedr nor any other WebSub-based push approach can be assumed to work across the whole source list.
- Did not research Feedbin API, Inoreader Business-tier API/webhooks, or RSS-Bridge as comparable alternatives — this key question's sub-asks on Feedbin/Inoreader/RSS-Bridge were not reached within the tool-call budget; flagging as an open gap for a follow-up pass if needed.

## Telegram: Bot API vs MTProto user-account libraries for monitoring many public channels

### Takeaway
The Bot API is architecturally unsuited for broad channel monitoring (bots can't proactively read arbitrary public channels they haven't been added to as admins, and the API is built around bots initiating/receiving user-opt-in interactions); MTProto client libraries (Telethon, Pyrogram, TDLib, GramJS) run as a real, logged-in user account exactly as the official Telegram app does, which is the legitimate, ToS-compliant way to join and read many public channels — but Telegram publishes no official numeric FLOOD_WAIT thresholds, so safe scaling to "hundreds" of channels has to be determined empirically via exponential backoff on FLOOD_WAIT errors rather than a documented request budget.

### Cited Findings
- A Telegram bot "cannot initiate a conversation with a user" and bot interactions are fundamentally opt-in/reactive, which constrains the Bot API's fit for proactive, broad channel monitoring compared to a user account — [Unipile: Telethon vs Telegram Bot API for Outreach](https://zupai.io/blog/telethon-vs-telegram-bot-api-for-outreach)
- MTProto libraries (Telethon et al.) "authenticate as real user accounts and can access what the official app provides," i.e., functionally identical capability to the first-party Telegram client — [zupai.io](https://zupai.io/blog/telethon-vs-telegram-bot-api-for-outreach)
- "Telegram publishes no limit for user accounts" — there is no official documented numeric FLOOD_WAIT/rate-limit table for MTProto user-account operations; the service instead returns a `FLOOD_WAIT_X` error at runtime specifying how many seconds to wait before retrying — [zupai.io](https://zupai.io/blog/telethon-vs-telegram-bot-api-for-outreach); confirmed independently by Pyrogram's own FAQ: "exact limits are unknown and can change anytime based on normal usages," with the prescribed remedy being to "slow things down and make less requests" and catch/sleep on the `FloodWait` exception — [Pyrogram docs: How to avoid Flood Waits](https://docs.pyrogram.org/faq/how-to-avoid-flood-waits)
- ToS/legitimacy framing: "Using it is not itself a violation. What triggers restrictions is behaviour: high volume, identical messages to strangers, and recipient reports" — i.e., the library choice (MTProto vs Bot API) is not itself the risk factor; it's send-like/spam-like behavior, which is not relevant to read-only channel monitoring — [zupai.io](https://zupai.io/blog/telethon-vs-telegram-bot-api-for-outreach)
- For the separate, better-documented Bot API (not directly what Simon would use for monitoring, but useful context), published guidance suggests roughly "no more than one message per second" within a given chat for outgoing messages, ~30 messages/second ceiling for bulk notifications across different chats, and a 429 Too Many Requests response mechanism with a `retry_after` value — [grammY flood control docs](https://grammy.dev/advanced/flood)

### Inferences
- Because Telegram does not publish numeric read/history-fetch limits for MTProto user sessions, the practical scaling approach for "hundreds" of channels is: join channels gradually (not all at once from a brand-new account, to avoid behavioral-anomaly flags), read channel history/new messages with built-in client-side pacing, and implement exponential backoff that honors the server's `FLOOD_WAIT_X` value exactly rather than guessing a budget — this is qualitatively different from GDELT/Cloudflare where documented numeric ceilings exist to design against.
- Reading public channels via MTProto as a real, logged-in account is the same mechanism the stock Telegram app uses, which supports the user's own framing that this is "Telegram's own intended, ToS-compliant way" — but because the actual constraint is behavioral/heuristic rather than numeric, scaling from 9 to "hundreds" of channels should be done incrementally with monitoring for `FLOOD_WAIT` responses as the feedback signal, not by assuming a fixed safe request-per-second number.
- Running a persistent MTProto session (Telethon/Pyrogram/GramJS) is architecturally different from Cloudflare Workers' stateless/short-lived execution model — Workers (including Durable Objects) are not designed for long-lived authenticated TCP/MTProto sessions, so this piece of the pipeline likely needs to live outside the Workers runtime proper (e.g., a small always-on process/container that periodically pushes parsed channel data into D1 via the Workers API, or a Durable Object that wakes on alarm to do short polling bursts rather than holding an open MTProto session). This is an architecture implication worth flagging explicitly to Simon since his stack is Workers-centric.

### Gaps
- Could not find Telegram's own official core.telegram.org documentation page directly (searches surfaced only third-party/community sources); the claim that "Telegram publishes no limit" is itself sourced from a third-party blog rather than confirmed by reading core.telegram.org directly — flagging this as not fully primary-sourced.
- Could not determine concrete per-session throughput Simon could expect in messages/channels-per-minute when doing bulk history backfill (`GetHistory`-style calls) across many channels — only qualitative "slow down, handle FloodWait" guidance was found, no empirical benchmark numbers.
- Did not confirm whether TDLib specifically publishes different/more conservative internal rate-limiting defaults than Telethon/Pyrogram — not reached within tool budget.

## GDELT: additional rate-limit-free bulk products beyond DOC 2.0 API and basic Event Export

### Takeaway
GDELT 2.0 ships three parallel bulk-file products updated every 15 minutes — Event Export, the Global Knowledge Graph (GKG), and the Mentions table — and all are static-file downloads (plus a BigQuery mirror), meaning Simon can ingest GKG and Mentions data with the same zero-rate-limit bulk-download pattern he already uses for Event Export, without touching the rate-limited live DOC 2.0 query API at all.

### Cited Findings
- GDELT 2.0 updates "every 15 minutes" across its core datasets, enabling near-real-time monitoring — [GDELT 2.0: Our Global World in Realtime (GDELT Project blog)](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/)
- The **Global Knowledge Graph (GKG)** is a distinct product from the Event Export files, connecting events to "people, organizations, locations, themes, emotions, counts, quotes" and tracking the broader "knowledge graph" context around events, not just the structured event records; it's available both as bulk files and in Google BigQuery for real-time querying — [GDELT Project blog](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/)
- The **Mentions table** tracks "every reference of an event over time," which lets you "assess the emotional reaction and resonance of an event as it sweeps through the world's media" — i.e., it captures repeat/secondary media mentions of an event beyond the single initiating record, which is additional volume/signal not present in the base Event Export file — [GDELT Project blog](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/)
- GDELT also publishes GKG data as GeoJSON files specifically for geographic/mapping use cases — [GDELT blog: "GDELT GKG GeoJSON Files Available"](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-gkg-geojson-files-available/)
- Access methods are bulk CSV master-file-list downloads (the same no-rate-limit static-file pattern as Event Export) or Google BigQuery for ad hoc querying of Events/Mentions/GKG tables — [GDELT Project blog](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/)

### Inferences
- Since Simon already has an Event Export ingestion pipeline built around 15-minute static files, extending it to also pull the GKG and Mentions files for the same 15-minute windows is largely an incremental engineering lift (same fetch/parse/schedule pattern, new file format/schema to parse) rather than new infrastructure — this is a low-to-medium effort way to multiply GDELT-derived volume and depth (thematic/tone context from GKG, media-resonance signal from Mentions) with **zero additional rate-limit exposure**, since these are the same unthrottled static-file downloads already in use for Event Export.
- The Mentions table in particular could directly support "social/media amplification" type signals (how much and how repeatedly an event got covered) which is differentiated from both the raw Event Export and the filtered live DOC API query currently budgeted at 20 req/5min — this could reduce reliance on the budget-capped DOC API for some use cases.

### Gaps
- Did not find specific file-size or row-count figures for GKG/Mentions 15-minute files that would let Simon estimate storage/parse cost in D1.
- Did not confirm whether GDELT 1.0 vs 2.0 schema/field differences matter for Simon's existing pipeline (he's presumably already on 2.0 given "GDELT live-query connector" and "Event Export" wording) — treated as out of scope since his existing connector already implies 2.0.
- Did not verify current GDELT bulk-file base URL/path conventions for GKG and Mentions (e.g., exact naming pattern analogous to the Event Export master file list) — would need a direct look at GDELT's data.html/documentation page to get exact download URL patterns before implementation.

## Multilingual translation at volume: Workers AI vs DeepL/Google/Azure

### Takeaway
Cloudflare Workers AI's m2m100-1.2b model is a many-to-many multilingual translator that runs inside the same Cloudflare account/billing as the rest of the stack at $0.342 per million input tokens and $0.342 per million output tokens, with no separate egress/API-key integration needed — likely the cheapest and most architecturally frictionless option for African-language press translation at volume, though exact language-pair coverage and quality versus DeepL/Google/Azure is unverified; among the external paid APIs, Azure AI Translator is the cheapest per-character ($10/million vs Google's $20/million and DeepL's ~$25-27.50/million above free tiers), but all three are new paid vendors and conflict with the "no new paid vendor" framing unless kept inside their free monthly allowances.

### Cited Findings
- Cloudflare Workers AI's `m2m100-1.2b` (Meta's "Many-to-Many" multilingual encoder-decoder translation model) is available via Workers AI, taking source/target language codes (e.g., 'en', 'es', 'fr') as parameters, and costs **$0.342 per million input tokens and $0.342 per million output tokens**, billed within the same Cloudflare account — [Cloudflare Workers AI docs: m2m100-1.2b](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/models/m2m100-1.2b/)
- DeepL API: Growth plan $26/month includes 1M characters then $27.50/additional million; API Pro tier is $5.49/month base plus $25/million above a 500K/month free allotment — [chatscontrol.com 2026 pricing comparison](https://chatscontrol.com/blog/translation-api-pricing-2026-deepl-google-azure) (secondary/aggregator source, not DeepL's own pricing page — treat as indicative, verify on deepl.com before committing)
- Google Cloud Translation: $20 per million characters after a 500K/month free tier — [chatscontrol.com](https://chatscontrol.com/blog/translation-api-pricing-2026-deepl-google-azure) (same caveat: secondary source)
- Azure AI Translator: $10 per million characters on the Standard S1 tier; free tier is 2 million characters/month but **only for the first year** of the Azure account — [chatscontrol.com](https://chatscontrol.com/blog/translation-api-pricing-2026-deepl-google-azure) (secondary source)
- Summary claim from the same secondary source: "Azure is half the cost of Google, and 2.5-3x cheaper than DeepL" at 5-10M monthly character volumes — [chatscontrol.com](https://chatscontrol.com/blog/translation-api-pricing-2026-deepl-google-azure)

### Inferences
- Workers AI's token-based pricing ($0.342/M tokens each way) is not directly comparable to the character-based pricing of DeepL/Google/Azure without knowing average tokens-per-character for the target languages (French/Portuguese/Arabic/Swahili/Amharic/Hausa), but given rough NMT tokenization rates (often ~1 token per 3-4 characters for Latin-script languages, likely worse for Arabic/Amharic's non-Latin scripts), Workers AI's effective per-character cost plausibly lands in a similar-to-cheaper range than Azure's $10/M characters — this needs a back-of-envelope recalculation once real sample text volumes are known, not assumed.
- Given the stated constraint of **no new paid data vendor**, Workers AI is the only option of the four that doesn't introduce a new vendor relationship at all (it's already inside Simon's existing Cloudflare billing) — this makes it the structurally preferred default to prototype first, reserving DeepL/Google/Azure as fallback-if-quality-insufficient options evaluated later, and only then within free-tier limits if "no new paid vendor" must hold strictly.
- m2m100 is an older-generation (circa 2020) multilingual NMT model; quality for lower-resource African languages (Hausa, Amharic, Swahili) versus more specialized/newer offerings from DeepL or Google is unverified and should be spot-checked against real press text before committing, especially since conflict-monitoring translation needs to preserve nuance (casualty figures, actor names, place names) reliably.

### Gaps
- Could not verify m2m100's actual supported language list/pairs (especially coverage and quality for Amharic and Hausa specifically, which are lower-resource languages even for large MT systems) — the Cloudflare docs page summary did not include an explicit language list.
- Pricing figures for DeepL/Google/Azure were sourced from a third-party aggregator (chatscontrol.com), not the vendors' own pricing pages — these should be verified directly on deepl.com/pro-api, cloud.google.com/translate/pricing, and azure.microsoft.com/pricing/details/cognitive-services/translator before being used for budgeting.
- Did not benchmark actual translation quality (BLEU-type comparisons or qualitative spot checks) for Workers AI m2m100 vs DeepL/Google/Azure on African-language news content specifically — this is a quality question, not a pricing one, and needs hands-on testing rather than documentation research.
