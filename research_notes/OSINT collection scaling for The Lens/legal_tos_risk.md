**Framing note:** This is general informational research for internal decision-making, not legal advice. Afrilens Consulting should seek a qualified attorney (ideally one familiar with both Kenyan data protection law and the jurisdictions of any target platforms/clients) before adopting any aggressive collection posture.

## Key Question 1: What is the current (2026) status and practical meaning of hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn, and how did the case actually end?

### Takeaway
hiQ "won" the famous 9th Circuit CFAA ruling (scraping purely public data isn't "hacking"), but it lost the underlying war: the case ultimately resolved in December 2022 with hiQ being permanently enjoined from scraping LinkedIn, ordered to destroy its scraped data/algorithms, and paying LinkedIn $500,000 — after a district court separately found hiQ *did* breach LinkedIn's contractual user agreement. The CFAA holding survives as precedent (at least in the 9th Circuit); the contract/ToS-breach outcome does not favor scrapers.

### Cited Findings
- The 9th Circuit held (across two rounds, 2019 and reaffirmed in 2022 after a remand from the Supreme Court's *Van Buren* decision) that scraping data a website intentionally makes publicly accessible, without a login wall, likely does not violate the CFAA because there's no "authorization" to circumvent. — [ZwillGen: hiQ v. LinkedIn Wrapped Up](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)
- Despite winning on CFAA, in November 2022 the district court ruled on remand that hiQ had separately *breached LinkedIn's User Agreement* through its scraping conduct (including by using automated/crowdsourced fake accounts to pull data). — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)
- The case concluded in December 2022 via a settlement/stipulated judgment: hiQ agreed to a **permanent injunction** against all scraping of LinkedIn, agreed to **delete its scraped data, source code, and algorithms**, and paid LinkedIn **$500,000** in damages. The settlement stipulation let LinkedIn "establish liability under the CFAA and California's state-law equivalent" for purposes of the judgment (though this doesn't set new binding precedent since it was a stipulation, not a litigated ruling). — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/); also reported by [Proskauer Rose](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case) and [Natlawreview](https://natlawreview.com/article/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-landmark-scraping-case)
- Practical upshot per legal commentary: the CFAA is a narrow, imperfect tool for platforms to stop scraping of genuinely public pages, but **breach-of-contract (ToS) claims remain a live and often-winning theory**, and companies can be held liable even where contractors/vendors did the actual scraping on their behalf. — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)
- Open/unresolved legal questions flagged by commentators post-settlement: what counts as "discovery of damage" for CFAA statute-of-limitations purposes, and how courts should evaluate technical "gating measures" (login walls, CAPTCHAs, IP-blocking) as evidence of lack of authorization. — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/)

### Inferences
- The popular narrative ("scraping public data is legal because of hiQ v. LinkedIn") is incomplete and risks giving false comfort: the CFAA holding is real, but it doesn't immunize a scraper from being sued and losing on breach-of-contract, and losing badly (injunction + $500K + forced data destruction) even as the "winning" party on the headline legal question.
- For a commercial OSINT consultancy, the CFAA piece is the least relevant protection in practice — contract law (ToS enforcement) is the mechanism platforms actually use to stop and punish scraping, and it worked against hiQ in the end.

### Gaps
- No single primary-source court opinion document was fetched directly (relied on secondary legal-blog summaries); the underlying district court order and settlement stipulation were not read in full text.

## Key Question 2: How does the hiQ precedent interact with platform ToS — can platforms still enforce bans/breach-of-contract claims — and what are the notable scraping lawsuits since (Meta, X/Twitter, LinkedIn)?

### Takeaway
Post-hiQ case law is genuinely mixed and trending toward *less* platform leverage via ToS specifically for **logged-out, no-account scraping of purely public data** — both Meta and X lost high-profile breach-of-contract claims against the scraper Bright Data in 2024 on the theory that ToS restrictions only bind "users" with accounts, not anonymous visitors. However, platforms still win decisively against scrapers who use **fake/compromised accounts, log in, or otherwise accept the ToS** — as LinkedIn has repeatedly shown, including as recently as 2026. Account-level and technical enforcement (bans, rate limits, IP blocks) remains fully available to platforms regardless of the civil-litigation outcome.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta Platforms v. Bright Data (N.D. Cal.)**: On January 23, 2024, Judge Edward Chen granted summary judgment for Bright Data, dismissing Meta's breach-of-contract claim. The court held Meta's anti-scraping terms apply to logged-in "users" exercising account privileges; because Bright Data scraped Instagram/Facebook public data without ever logging in or using an account, it wasn't bound by those terms, no differently than any anonymous site visitor. — [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-1470808/); also [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/federal-judge-rules-against-meta-in-data-scraping-case/)
- Meta subsequently **dropped** its (remaining) lawsuit against Bright Data in February 2024 rather than continue litigating. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/02/26/meta-drops-lawsuit-against-web-scraping-firm-bright-data-that-sold-millions-of-instagram-records)
- **X Corp v. Bright Data (N.D. Cal., Judge William Alsup)**: In a May/June 2024 ruling, the court dismissed X's claim that Bright Data's scraping and resale of publicly available X data breached X's Terms of Service. The court applied a **copyright-preemption** theory: because (a) X doesn't own copyright in the scraped posts (its own users do) and (b) X's ToS banned scraping "irrespective of whether it qualifies as fair use," enforcing the ToS would conflict with federal copyright policy (fair-use rights of X's own users), so state contract law was preempted. — [Eric Goldman's Technology & Marketing Law Blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/05/x-corp-v-bright-data-is-the-decision-weve-been-waiting-for-guest-blog-post.htm); confirmed by [Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240604-california-federal-court-holds-x-s-claims) and [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/x-corp-loses-battle-over-public-data-access)
- Commentary notes this is a *more durable* defeat for platforms than the Meta ruling because it rests on federal preemption doctrine (hard to route around via better-drafted ToS language), rather than a narrower "you weren't a logged-in user" contract-interpretation gap that a platform could potentially close by rewriting its terms. — [Eric Goldman's blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/05/x-corp-v-bright-data-is-the-decision-weve-been-waiting-for-guest-blog-post.htm)
- Bloomberg Law reported X's lawsuit was **partially revived** on some other claims even as the core ToS/contract theory was rejected — indicating the case was not a total loss for X on every count. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/x-partially-revives-lawsuit-against-israeli-data-scraping-firm) (headline-level; full article not fetched)
- **LinkedIn's subsequent scraping enforcement remains aggressive and largely successful where fake/automated accounts are used.** LinkedIn has sued multiple scraping companies; one 2026 case (LinkedIn v. ProAPIs/Netswift) ended in a **consent judgment imposing a permanent ban and a data-destruction order** against the defendants over "industrial scale" member-data scraping via fake accounts. — [SigmaLawGroup summary](https://sigmalawgroup.com/blog/2026-09-18-linkedin-proapis-scraping-consent-judgment/); also reported by [The Record](https://therecord.media/linkedin-sues-data-scraping-company) and [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-wins-legal-case-data-scrapers-proxycurl/756101/) (LinkedIn vs. Proxycurl)
- General pattern across these cases: **the critical legal fact is whether the scraper logged in / used an account (and thus accepted the ToS as a contracting party) versus scraped purely public, logged-out pages.** Logged-out scraping of public pages is where platforms have been losing contract claims (Meta, X); account-based/fake-account scraping is where platforms keep winning decisively (LinkedIn's multiple suits).

### Inferences
- The "logged-in vs. logged-out" line is now the single most important practical distinction in US platform-scraping litigation. A commercial OSINT firm that (a) never creates or uses platform accounts to collect data, and (b) only reads pages a platform serves without authentication, is operating in the legally strongest position currently recognized by US courts — though this is a fast-moving, still-developing area (several of these rulings are recent, under appeal, or not yet final).
- Breach-of-contract/ToS risk is highest, and courts most sympathetic to platforms, when: the scraper creates accounts (real or fake) to get around access limits, uses compromised/purchased credentials, or otherwise manifests agreement to a clickwrap/ToS that it then violates.
- Even where a platform loses a civil lawsuit on the contract theory, nothing in these rulings constrains the platform's unilateral, extra-judicial enforcement (IP bans, rate-limiting, account termination) — litigation loss ≠ loss of practical ability to block or throttle a scraper.
- Legal outcomes here are jurisdiction- and fact-specific (Northern District of California, product of particular ToS language and particular scraping methods); they are persuasive but not a blanket national rule, and definitely don't bind courts outside the US.

### Gaps
- Did not confirm final/appellate status of X Corp v. Bright Data as of October 2026 (one source mentions a case-tracker suggesting ongoing status into 2026, but full current posture wasn't verified: [ailawsuittracker.com](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/x-corp-v-bright-data-ltd-3-23-cv-03698-wha/) was found in search but not fetched for full content).
- Did not verify whether Meta or X has since amended their ToS language specifically to try to close the "not a logged-in user" loophole identified by these rulings.
- Reddit-specific scraping litigation (e.g., Reddit's known disputes with AI companies/data brokers over API access and scraping) was referenced in the assignment but not independently researched in this pass — flagging as a gap for the report writer if Reddit-specific case law is needed.

## Key Question 3: EU/UK and Kenyan data-protection landscape for scraping and storing personal data (including public posts); EU sui generis database rights

### Takeaway
Scraping personal data — including data that is technically "public" on social media — is treated by EU regulators as presumptively **unlawful absent a valid legal basis**, and "it's public" is explicitly rejected as a defense; Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 imposes a GDPR-like lawful-basis and registration framework that would apply to Afrilens's own processing regardless of where the data subjects or platforms are based. Separately, the EU's *sui generis* database right can give EU-based content/database owners a distinct intellectual-property claim against bulk extraction and re-use of their databases, independent of copyright or contract.

### Cited Findings
- A Dutch Data Protection Authority (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens) position, summarized by a privacy-law commentary site, states that web scraping of personal data is "almost always unlawful under the GDPR" because organizations generally cannot obtain the advance consent GDPR requires, and that "legitimate interest" as a legal basis is "severely restricted" — scraping cannot be justified by the sole purpose of making money from the resulting data. — [Aphaia](https://aphaia.co.uk/web-scraping-is-almost-always-unlawful-under-the-gdpr/)
- The same guidance explicitly rejects the "it's public, so it's fair game" argument: "the fact that information about you is public does not automatically mean that you also give permission for scraping" — public availability makes data technically accessible, not lawfully collectible. — [Aphaia](https://aphaia.co.uk/web-scraping-is-almost-always-unlawful-under-the-gdpr/)
- The DPA guidance cited allows only narrow exceptions: purely domestic/hobby use shared with a small circle, or a highly targeted business use such as a company monitoring public mentions of itself — a scenario notably narrower than a commercial OSINT consultancy's general-purpose social-media/news monitoring business. — [Aphaia](https://aphaia.co.uk/web-scraping-is-almost-always-unlawful-under-the-gdpr/)
- Separately, EU regulators have in 2024 taken a "restrictive GDPR position on data scraping" specifically in the context of AI training data, according to law firm Morgan Lewis — reinforcing that the EU regulatory trend is toward tighter scrutiny of scraping generally, not looser. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/05/eu-regulator-adopts-restrictive-gdpr-position-on-data-scraping-impacting-ai-technologies) (headline-level; not independently fetched for full detail)
- **Kenya's Data Protection Act No. 24 of 2019** requires any processing of personal data to rest on one of several lawful grounds under Section 30 (consent, contract necessity, legal obligation, vital interests, public-interest task, or legitimate interests of the controller/third party). — [Dimeri.ai summary of the Act](https://www.dimeri.ai/blog/guides/data-protection-act-kenya); primary text available at [Kenya Law](http://kenyalaw.org/kl/fileadmin/pdfdownloads/Acts/2019/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) and [new.kenyalaw.org](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2019/24/eng@2022-12-31) (not independently fetched in full in this pass)
- Under Section 18 of the Act, **every data controller and data processor must register with the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) before processing**, with non-registration treated as a criminal offense. — [Dimeri.ai](https://www.dimeri.ai/blog/guides/data-protection-act-kenya)
- Cross-border transfer of personal data out of Kenya (Section 48) requires either an adequacy finding for the receiving country, Commissioner-approved safeguards, or data-subject consent. — [Dimeri.ai](https://www.dimeri.ai/blog/guides/data-protection-act-kenya)
- Penalties for non-compliance can reach **KES 5 million or 1% of annual turnover** for controllers, with potential criminal imprisonment (up to 10 years cited) for certain offenses such as unlawful processing without registration. — [Dimeri.ai](https://www.dimeri.ai/blog/guides/data-protection-act-kenya)
- The secondary source consulted did **not** identify a specific journalism/public-interest or research exemption in the Kenyan Act comparable to GDPR's journalism derogations — this should be treated as unconfirmed rather than a finding that no such exemption exists (see Gaps).
- On **EU sui generis database rights** (Database Directive 96/9/EC): search results confirm this is an active, litigated area distinct from copyright — the CJEU has ruled on the scope of the "sui generis right of the database maker" (e.g., case C-762/19, discussed by Lexology) and France has pursued scraping defendants specifically on database-right grounds (Hogan Lovells). — [Lexology on CJEU C-762/19](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=6f084811-5c41-4bbb-af1a-6ee3936122e3); [Hogan Lovells on French database-scraping enforcement](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/france-protecting-a-website-from-unlawful-data-scraping); overview at [EUR-Lex: Legal protection of databases](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/summary/legal-protection-databases.html)
- A Stanford Law School working paper specifically addresses "Legality and Challenges of Web Scraping Databases in the European Union: Focus on Non-Personal Data and Copyright," indicating this is recognized as a live, unsettled academic/legal question in the EU context. — [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/no-124-legality-and-challenges-of-web-scraping-databases-in-the-european-union-focus-on-non-personal-data-and-copyright/) (title/abstract-level; full paper not fetched)

### Inferences
- If Afrilens stores and republishes scraped social media/news content as part of a paid intelligence product, and that product has EU clients, is accessible to EU-based users, or involves data about EU data subjects, it should assume **both** (a) GDPR exposure for processing personal data without a clear lawful basis, and (b) potential sui generis database-right exposure if the source is a structured database protected under EU law (this is distinct from, and in addition to, ordinary copyright in individual posts).
- The "public post" defense that is weak in US contract-law terms (Meta/X rulings) is essentially *absent* as a defense under EU/Dutch DPA-style GDPR reasoning — these are two different legal systems reaching different practical answers to "can you collect public posts," and Afrilens should not assume a US court win on ToS translates to EU/GDPR safety.
- Kenya's own DPA 2019 creates a baseline compliance obligation for Afrilens as a Kenya-based controller/processor *independent of* what any foreign platform's ToS says — registration with the ODPC and identifying a lawful basis (most plausibly "legitimate interests" for professional security-monitoring purposes) is a Kenya-law compliance item regardless of the scraping-ToS question entirely.

### Gaps
- Did not confirm whether Kenya's DPA 2019 contains an explicit research/journalism/public-interest exemption analogous to GDPR Article 85 — the one source checked didn't address this, and primary statutory text was not read directly in this pass.
- Did not fetch the GDPR Article 85/journalism-exemption analysis in the EU context, nor case-specific guidance from ICO (UK) on scraping — the UK ICO's position (distinct from an EU member state DPA) was not independently researched; this is a notable gap since the research brief asked about EU/UK specifically.
- Did not confirm the current/2026 relevance of the ResearchGate paper "To Scrape or Not to Scrape? The Lawfulness of Social Media Crawling under the GDPR" beyond its title appearing in search results — not fetched.
- Sui generis database right findings here are necessarily general; whether any *specific* platform Afrilens would scrape (X, Meta, Telegram, specific African news outlets) has a database that would actually qualify for EU sui generis protection (requires "substantial investment" in obtaining/verifying/presenting the contents) was not analyzed platform-by-platform.

## Key Question 4: Practical enforcement reality — what actually happens to accounts/IPs that scrape aggressively against ToS, and how do platforms detect/respond in 2025-2026?

### Takeaway
Independent of any lawsuit, platforms maintain and actively use a graduated technical-enforcement toolkit — rate-limiting, CAPTCHA/JS challenges, shadow-limiting, IP and ASN blocking, device/behavioral fingerprinting, and account suspension or permanent ban — and a substantial commercial industry (anti-bot vendors, proxy/evasion vendors) exists on both sides of this arms race, which itself confirms detection and enforcement are routine and ongoing in 2025-2026, not theoretical.

### Cited Findings
- Multiple 2025-2026 industry guides (from scraping-tool vendors themselves) describe current anti-bot defenses including IP reputation/ASN blocking, TLS/browser fingerprinting, behavioral analysis, and challenge pages (e.g., Cloudflare, Imperva/Incapsula), and discuss the need for "proxy strategy," residential IP rotation, and anti-detection browser techniques specifically to evade these measures — confirming these defenses are live and actively deployed by major platforms. — [ScrapingAnt: Proxy Strategy in 2025](https://scrapingant.com/blog/proxy-strategy-in-2025-beating-anti-bot-systems-without); [Scrapfly: Bypassing Imperva Incapsula 2026](https://scrapfly.io/blog/posts/how-to-bypass-imperva-incapsula-anti-scraping); [Rebrowser: Anti-Scraping Protection in 2025](https://rebrowser.net/blog/anti-scraping-protection-from-basic-defense-to-advanced-implementation); [ScraperAPI: Bypassing Anti-Bot Detection](https://www.scraperapi.com/blog/bypassing-anti-bot-detection/)
- The existence of an entire "won't get you banned" genre of vendor content (e.g., round-ups of "social media scrapers that won't get you banned") is itself evidence that **account bans for scraping-like automated behavior are a known, common outcome** platforms impose, serious enough to be a primary marketing angle for scraping tool vendors. — [Thunderbit: 11 Best Social Media Scrapers That Won't Get You Banned](https://thunderbit.com/blog/best-social-media-scrapers)
- LinkedIn's litigation record (see Key Question 2) shows platforms pair legal action with account-level consequences: the 2026 LinkedIn v. ProAPIs/Netswift consent judgment included a **permanent ban plus data-destruction order**, i.e., both a court-ordered prohibition and (implicitly) platform-side account/access termination. — [SigmaLawGroup](https://sigmalawgroup.com/blog/2026-09-18-linkedin-proapis-scraping-consent-judgment/)
- Residential-proxy/IP-rotation services (Bright Data, Oxylabs) are explicitly marketed as tools to defeat these exact defenses (IP-based blocking in particular) — reinforcing that IP blocking is a primary, expected, and actively-circumvented enforcement mechanism. — [ScrapingAnt](https://scrapingant.com/blog/proxy-strategy-in-2025-beating-anti-bot-systems-without); general proxy-vendor marketing context from [Bright Data vs Oxylabs comparison](https://use-apify.com/blog/bright-data-vs-oxylabs-2026)

### Inferences
- Even where a scraper has a plausible legal defense (e.g., logged-out, public-page-only scraping like Bright Data's winning posture against Meta/X), platforms retain full practical freedom to detect and block that activity technically — a legal win on ToS-breach liability does not equal a right to uninterrupted access. For an OSINT consultancy, this means operational risk (losing access, having to constantly adapt to blocks) exists independent of legal risk, and is likely to be the more immediate, recurring cost of aggressive scraping.
- The vendor marketing language itself ("won't get you banned," tools to "beat anti-bot systems") is a signal of risk, not reassurance: if evasion is actively necessary and marketed as such, aggressive scraping against a platform's technical and contractual wishes is the norm being evaded, not an edge case.

### Gaps
- Did not find platform-specific (X, Meta, LinkedIn, Reddit) official statements or transparency-report-style data quantifying how many accounts/IPs are banned for scraping in 2025-2026, or describing their detection methodology in their own words (as opposed to third-party/vendor commentary) — this would strengthen the report but requires fetching each platform's current ToS/anti-automation policy pages directly, which the time/call budget for this research pass did not include.
- Did not separately verify Reddit's specific 2023-2026 API/scraping enforcement actions (e.g., its well-known 2023 API pricing change and subsequent crackdown on third-party scraping), which the research brief flagged as a named example — flagging as a gap for the report writer.

## Key Question 5: Does a commercial OSINT company face different/higher risk than an academic researcher doing the same scraping? What's the state of law on storing/republishing scraped content for a paid B2B product, and the copyright/database-rights angle?

### Takeaway
The research surfaced strong indirect support that commercial purpose and republication materially increase legal exposure beyond "mere reading/collection," through two independent doctrines: (1) Dutch GDPR guidance explicitly singles out making money as disqualifying scraping from the "legitimate interest" legal basis; and (2) in the US, the X Corp v. Bright Data court's reasoning turned partly on *resale* of scraped data and fair-use/copyright dynamics that are directly implicated by republishing content, not just collecting it. Direct case law contrasting "academic researcher" vs. "commercial company" defendants specifically was not found in this research pass and is flagged as a gap.

### Cited Findings
- Dutch DPA guidance (as summarized) explicitly states legitimate-interest cannot justify scraping done "for the sole purpose of making money," directly implicating any commercial monetization of scraped personal data as a weaker legal position than a non-commercial use. — [Aphaia](https://aphaia.co.uk/web-scraping-is-almost-always-unlawful-under-the-gdpr/)
- The Bright Data cases involved a company that scrapes *and resells* data as a core commercial business (a "data-as-a-service" proxy/dataset vendor) — commentary treats resale/redistribution as a central fact pattern in both the Meta and X cases, and Oxylabs' own dataset-services contract terms (see Key Question 3 section) impose extensive liability-shifting and compliance obligations on customers specifically because reselling/republishing scraped datasets raises downstream legal-use issues the vendor wants no part of. — [Oxylabs Dataset General Conditions](https://oxylabs.io/legal/oxylabs-dataset-general-conditions); case context from [JD Supra on Meta v. Bright Data](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-1470808/)
- Academic/non-commercial researchers are commonly cited in broader scraping-law commentary (e.g., journalistic investigation guides) as benefiting from different normative and sometimes legal treatment (e.g., GDPR's research/archiving-friendly provisions, fair-use leanings in copyright analysis) — a Markup piece specifically framed around "how to legally scrape EU data for investigations" suggests a public-interest/journalistic lens exists as a distinct, more favorable framework, though this piece's content was not fetched in full to confirm specifics. — [The Markup: How to Legally Scrape EU Data for Investigations](https://themarkup.org/levelup/2023/08/23/how-to-legally-scrape-eu-data-for-investigations) (title/context-level only; not independently fetched)
- The Oxylabs contract's indemnification clause (Section 7) requiring customers to "defend and indemnify Oxylabs against any third-party claim" arising from the customer's use of datasets in breach of applicable law directly evidences that **downstream commercial use/republication, not mere collection, is where the vendor itself identifies the greatest legal risk** — it declines to accept that risk itself and contractually pushes it onto the paying customer. — [Oxylabs Dataset General Conditions](https://oxylabs.io/legal/oxylabs-dataset-general-conditions)

### Inferences
- A commercial OSINT consultancy that stores and republishes (even in processed/analyzed form) scraped social media content to paying clients is taking on meaningfully more legal risk than either (a) a researcher who collects data transiently for analysis without redistributing the raw underlying content, or (b) a consumer who merely reads public pages — both under EU GDPR's commercial-purpose skepticism and under the copyright/resale dynamics central to the Bright Data cases.
- Using a third-party scraping/proxy vendor (Bright Data, Oxylabs) does not eliminate this risk for Afrilens — these vendors' own contracts explicitly disclaim liability and shift it contractually to the customer, meaning Afrilens would bear the legal exposure for how it uses the data regardless of whose infrastructure performed the scraping.
- No evidence was found of a specific carve-out or heightened-risk doctrine that names "commercial company" as a defendant category distinct from "academic researcher" in a scraping-specific statute or case; the risk differential found here is *functional* (commercial legitimate-interest limits under GDPR, resale/redistribution fact patterns in US cases) rather than a formal doctrinal line. The report writer should characterize this as "commercial, redistributive use faces materially higher practical risk" rather than claiming a hard legal rule exists specifically for "companies vs. academics."

### Gaps
- No case law was found (in this research pass) directly adjudicating "is an academic researcher treated differently in law than a commercial company for the same scraping conduct" — this remains an inference from adjacent doctrine (GDPR legitimate-interest commercial-purpose limits, copyright fair-use commercial-vs-non-commercial factor analysis generally) rather than a directly cited holding.
- Did not fetch GDPR's research/archiving exemptions (Article 89 and related recitals) directly to confirm their scope and whether/how they could apply to a commercial security-monitoring product that claims a research or public-interest angle.
- Did not find data on actual copyright infringement lawsuits specifically over *republication* of scraped individual social media posts (as opposed to bulk database scraping) — this is a plausible additional risk vector (ordinary copyright in each post, held by its author) that overlaps with but is distinct from the sui generis database right, and deserves separate legal review for Afrilens's specific product design (e.g., does it quote/republish verbatim post text, or only extracted facts/metadata).

## Overall Synthesis Note for Report Writer

Three independent, non-overlapping legal frameworks all point toward the same operational conclusion, from different angles:
1. **US CFAA** — largely neutralized as a weapon against logged-out scraping of public data (hiQ, reaffirmed), but irrelevant comfort because:
2. **US breach-of-contract/ToS law** — now the primary battleground; currently *favors* pure logged-out public scraping (Meta v. Bright Data, X v. Bright Data) but punishes account-based/fake-account/login-based scraping severely (hiQ's final settlement, LinkedIn's multiple wins) — and is fact-specific, unsettled, and jurisdiction-bound (US federal courts only; irrelevant to how a Kenyan court or an EU regulator would treat the same conduct).
3. **EU/Kenya data-protection law** — essentially indifferent to the ToS/CFAA question entirely; treats collection and storage of personal data (including from public posts) as needing its own independent lawful basis, with commercial monetization specifically weakening that basis, plus a wholly separate EU sui generis database-right exposure for bulk extraction from protected databases.

None of these frameworks, even where most favorable, affects a platform's unilateral technical enforcement (rate-limits, IP/account bans) — which operates as a practical business-continuity risk regardless of the legal merits. The combination of (a) unsettled/adverse contract law the moment login or fake accounts are involved, (b) adverse data-protection exposure specifically for commercial, redistributive, public-social-media collection, (c) Kenya's own independent DPA 2019 compliance obligations as a controller, and (d) the practical certainty of technical detection/enforcement, together support a recommendation against evasive or aggressive scraping (fake accounts, login-based automation, proxy-based IP-rotation evasion against a platform's explicit anti-automation terms) as a business practice for Afrilens/The Lens, in favor of: public no-login data sources with minimal technical evasion (e.g., Telegram's public preview pages, public RSS, official APIs where available), explicit lawful-basis and DPA-registration compliance under Kenya's Act, and caution about verbatim republication of scraped content to paying clients.
